The agent finishes the report in six minutes. The analyst spends an hour checking it. Another half hour goes into fixing the period labels and finding two missing statements.

If the old process took two hours, the useful comparison is two hours against the full new process. Measuring generation alone would hide most of the work.

This is an illustrative example, but it describes the measurement we build into a Recoup pilot: follow the output through acceptance. Preparation, checking, corrections, and exceptions belong in the same record as the AI run.

## Define the unit before timing it

One “report” can cover a single catalog or a portfolio. One “deal reviewed” can mean a first read or a completed investment memo. A timing comparison needs the same kind of work on both sides.

For a reporting pilot, we record the reporting period, number of input files, missing information, and accepted output. We compare a set of similar runs and keep difficult cases visible. The owner should be able to see whether a result changed because the workflow improved or because the second batch was easier.

We also record waiting separately from hands-on time. A system may shorten a two-day wait for an answer without reducing two days of labor. Faster turnaround can still matter; it needs the right label.

## Give recovered time somewhere to go

Suppose a team completes 40 comparable reports a month. In a planning scenario, the old process takes 120 minutes per report and the tested process takes 90 minutes, including review. That difference would recover 20 hours across the monthly volume.

Those 20 hours are available capacity. The owner still needs to decide what happens to them. Perhaps the team clears an overdue exceptions queue, answers more artist questions, or reviews another catalog before a deadline.

Record that decision before expansion. Then check whether the work happened. Otherwise, a report about “hours saved” tells leadership little about what changed for the business.

Our [ROI planner](/roi) helps work through assumptions. Keep its projections separate from measured results, and include setup, licenses, integration, training, review, and ongoing maintenance in the cost of the change.

## Confirm cash separately

If the payroll, contractor bill, and other expenses remain unchanged, recovered hours do not create an equivalent cash saving.

A budget owner may be able to confirm a canceled external expense or a planned hire that is no longer required for the same workload. That is a separate financial fact. It needs a period, an amount, and evidence that the expense was reduced or avoided.

Avoid counting the same benefit twice. If an analyst uses recovered hours to support more deals, the team can describe the extra capacity and investigate its commercial effect. It should not also claim the analyst's unchanged salary as cash removed from the cost base.

## Match the measure to the uncertainty

Sometimes the first test exists to answer a capability question. Can the workflow identify the relevant clauses across the team's document formats? Can it distinguish a missing statement from a reporting delay? In that case, the first result is a record of accepted examples and failure types.

A commercial pilot needs a different observation. If a catalog team tests better sync shortlists, count the briefs received, the shortlists accepted, and the paid outcomes. Plan a comparison before the trial where practical. Track changes in demand, catalog availability, pricing, and sales effort that might also explain the result.

Bookings, signed licenses, and cash received can happen in different periods. Keep those dates separate. A promising shortlist is evidence about the workflow; it is not a completed sale.

## Put the decision next to the evidence

The pilot review should end with a specific next commitment: expand to another operator, repair one failure and retest, keep the current scope, or stop.

We attach the evidence to that decision. The owner can see the task volume, total human time, quality checks, full costs, and unresolved assumptions. A small sample can justify another test while leaving a larger financial claim open.
