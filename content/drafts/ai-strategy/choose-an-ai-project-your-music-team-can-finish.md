A catalog report can arrive on time and still leave finance chasing the answer it needed. The spreadsheet is finished. The unmatched records are buried in a tab. Someone has to open the files again before the meeting.

That is a useful place to begin an AI project. There is a recurring job, a recipient, and a specific piece of work left undone.

At Recoup, we start by naming the output that would change that person's week. For this example, it might be a checked list of unmatched records, with the supporting files and a reason each needs attention. Once the team agrees on that result, we can decide which parts should use AI, ordinary software, or a person's judgment.

## Follow the work to the person who uses it

“Automate reporting” leaves too much open. A finance lead, a catalog administrator, and an investor can all mean different things by a report.

We ask who receives the output and what they do next. A useful answer sounds like: “The catalog administrator needs to identify the ten records to investigate before finance closes the period.” That defines the work more precisely than a list of software features.

Then we look at a completed example. What did the team receive? What did they change? Which questions required another person? A task that looks repetitive from a distance can contain difficult decisions about recording identity, reporting periods, or conflicting rights information.

The first project should make one of those decisions easier to reach. Its output needs to be something the recipient can accept or reject.

## Check whether the team can run the test

A convincing demonstration can use a clean file that never appears in the team's weekly work. We want the first test to include the files the team is permitted to use, the awkward cases it encounters, and the person who checks the result.

For a catalog exceptions report, that means finding a representative reporting period, confirming access to the required exports, and agreeing on what counts as a valid match. If the reviewer cannot verify a record, the result must preserve that uncertainty.

These requirements remain visible in the project brief. Missing access and an absent reviewer are separate problems. They need separate owners and actions; a strong commercial case cannot resolve either one by itself.

Some useful projects will therefore wait. While access is being arranged, another team may already have a smaller task with a clear owner and examples ready to inspect. That can be the better first commitment.

## Include the work already happening

Before adding another tool, we ask what people already use to complete the job. Someone may have a spreadsheet rule, a saved prompt, or a small script that handles part of it.

Keep the parts that work. Find out who maintains them and which cases still require intervention. If two people built similar methods, compare their outputs on the same example before choosing one to develop.

The purpose of this review is a clear decision about effort. A useful existing workflow may need documentation and a second trained operator. A fragile workflow may need a narrower scope. Both decisions can be more useful than starting another build.

## Write the review before the build

For our reporting example, the brief names an owner, a review date, and the evidence to collect. The team records time spent preparing, generating, checking, and correcting the report. It also records whether exceptions were found correctly and whether the recipient could use the result.

We agree on what would justify continuing and what would make us revise or stop. Those conditions should describe the business's tolerance for mistakes. A faster report that quietly confirms an uncertain rights match has failed a different test from one that correctly flags the uncertainty but needs a formatting fix.

The first commitment can be small. A few representative runs may establish whether the approach deserves a larger trial. They do not establish an annual return or authorize wider access.
