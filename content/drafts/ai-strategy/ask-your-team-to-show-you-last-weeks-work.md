A release manager says approvals take too long. Marketing says it sends the assets on time. Both can be right: the approval request may arrive without the version, territory, or usage information the reviewer needs.

A faster reminder would move the incomplete request through the same loop again.

At Recoup, we ask the team to bring a recent piece of work and walk us through it. We want to see the request, the information available at that moment, the handoff, and the corrections. That sequence helps us identify what a useful system should change.

## Give people a specific job to describe

We use a short team worksheet to collect examples before deciding what to build. It asks for one recurring job, the person who receives the result, the steps involved, and the problems encountered on a recent run.

The scope matters. “Campaign operations” can cover dozens of decisions. “Get the approved cover asset to the distributor” gives the conversation a start and an end.

For that task, we would ask which asset was requested, where the current version lived, who could approve it, what information accompanied the request, and what caused it to come back. We would also ask what the distributor accepted. Finishing an internal checklist and completing the external handoff are different events.

## Ask the recipient too

The person preparing the work knows where the effort goes. The recipient knows what makes the result usable. We include both, plus someone who handles exceptions when the normal process breaks.

An operator may report that copying information takes the most time. The reviewer may explain that checking which version is authoritative creates the delay. Those observations can lead to different interventions.

We preserve the disagreement until an example resolves it. If the team has no reliable way to identify the approved version, that becomes a requirement for the project. It should not disappear into a general recommendation to “improve data quality.”

## Count the work without inventing precision

A useful discovery record separates counted activity from recollection. “We handled 24 requests in September” should point to the record used to count them. “It usually takes about an hour” remains an estimate until someone measures comparable tasks.

Record preparation, execution, review, and corrections separately. Record waiting time separately too. Include the time of everyone involved, without counting the same person-minute twice.

This gives the team a way to choose the next observation. If nobody knows how often the job happens, count it for an agreed period. If the expensive part is rework, sample returned requests and record why they came back.

## Find the methods people already trust

Ask what has already been tried. A saved prompt may work well for standard releases and fail on collaborations. A spreadsheet may contain rules that only one person understands. A failed automation may have lost access to a file it needed.

Those details change the recommendation. The saved prompt might need better examples. The spreadsheet might need documentation and a second owner. The automation might need a reliable access arrangement before any new AI work begins.

We also ask what makes people hesitant to use a new process. A workflow that requires a release manager to inspect another dashboard during delivery week has a practical adoption cost. Include that cost in the design and the first test.

## Be clear about who sees the answers

Before collecting responses, name the project team, explain the purpose, and decide where the records will live. Tell people whether names are required and who can read individual answers. A form that records identities should never promise anonymity.

We keep confidential files in the approved workspace and collect only what the investigation needs. The public worksheet can use role names and general task descriptions. Specific artist, contract, and financial records can be reviewed through the client's authorized process when needed.

## Leave with one testable change

For the release example, the first change might be a request that gathers the required asset information, checks for missing fields, and prepares a review packet. The reviewer still approves it. The test measures whether requests come back less often and whether the accepted handoff takes less total effort.
