# Discovery, proposals, and follow-up

Adapted from the consulting practice on 2026-09-14. These are working methods, not promises about results or delivery terms.

## Learn the actual problem

Ask the buyer to walk through a recent example. Learn what arrives, who handles it, which tools they use, where it breaks, and what they do next. Ask:

- What is at stake if this stays the same?
- What have you already tried, and why did it fall short?
- Who does the work, who approves a change, and who controls the budget?
- What would a useful improvement look like, and how would you measure it?
- What data, access, timing, and budget constraints must we work within?

Listen for a recurring task with an owner and consequences. “We should use AI” needs further discovery. An apparent technical solution is a hypothesis until you understand the workflow and the buyer's constraints.

If the call is recorded, use the agreed recording process. Keep private recordings and notes in approved private storage. Verify consequential quotations, amounts, dates, and speaker attribution against the actual recording or transcript; an AI meeting summary is a lead to evidence, not final proof.

## Leave a short record

Record the current problem in the buyer's words, a specific example, the consequences, desired result, decision participants, constraints, open questions, and the next step actually agreed. Cite the source privately.

Separate customer statements from your interpretation. Mark a success measure as **proposed** until the buyer agrees. Do not invent a budget, approval, deadline, or meeting to make the record look complete. Keep deal state in the CRM rather than a second competing spreadsheet.

## Write the proposal around the result

Use this order:

1. The situation and why it matters now.
2. The desired result and agreed success measures.
3. The approach, scope, boundaries, and dependencies.
4. Each side's responsibilities and the handoff.
5. Price, payment schedule, support, and other agreed terms.
6. The next decision or review step.

If options help, vary meaningful scope: advice, implementation, and ongoing service require different work. Explain the difference plainly. Do not fabricate a price anchor or use inherited template figures as current Recoup pricing. If price changes, state the corresponding change in scope, support, or terms.

Only promise ownership, access, support levels, delivery dates, or outcomes that the applicable agreement supports. Proposed terms are proposals until accepted.

## Follow up usefully

Summarize what mattered to the buyer, address a real open question, share a directly relevant resource, or confirm the next agreed step. Keep the message short. Drafts must not claim a meeting is booked or work has begun unless it has.

When a buyer hesitates, ask what remains unclear and what criteria matter to the decision. A missing problem owner, vague stakes, absent budget, or incompatible timing can mean the opportunity is not ready. Record that honestly. Respect declines and stop follow-up when the context no longer supports contact.
